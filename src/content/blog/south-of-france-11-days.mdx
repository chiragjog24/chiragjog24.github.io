In three weeks we head to the South of France for eleven days — Mumbai to Paris to Nice on Air France, then a TGV south to Provence, four nights at a Relais & Châteaux country house outside Avignon, back to the Riviera for the Monaco Grand Prix weekend. Two halves of southern France stitched together by a single train ride.

Some trips ask you to choose: rural France or the Riviera, slow countryside or Monte Carlo glitz. This one says no. Both are worth seeing, and they're three hours apart by direct train.

## Why the South of France

The South of France isn't one place. It's two — and they don't look like each other.

**Provence** is rural and slow. Stone villages perched on hilltops since the 12th century. Vineyards in long rows running up to Mont Ventoux. Saturday markets in town squares where the same families have been selling olive oil for four generations. Long lunches that start at 12:30 and aren't seriously expecting you to leave before 3:00. The Romans built it, the Popes ran it for seventy years in the 14th century, the Provençaux have been making rosé out of it ever since.

**The Riviera** is coastal and theatrical. Belle Époque hotels still standing on the Promenade des Anglais. Casinos and superyachts. The Mediterranean shading from cobalt to turquoise depending on the angle of light. A railway built in the 1860s connects all the famous coastal towns — Cannes, Nice, Villefranche, Beaulieu, Èze, Cap Ferrat, Monaco — at €4 a ticket and 7 minutes apart.

Both are worth a trip. We're going to do both.

## Provence — the slow half

![Provençal lavender at sunset, Valensole](https://images.pexels.com/photos/33486886/pexels-photo-33486886.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1600)

We're basing four nights at **Auberge de Cassagne & Spa**, a 5* Relais & Châteaux country house eight kilometres from Avignon TGV. Two restaurants (one Michelin-starred), full spa with hammam, indoor and outdoor pools, gardens, vineyard views. The kind of hotel that's a destination by itself — you don't need to leave to feel like you've travelled somewhere.

The plan inside Provence:

**Day one** is the spa day. Pool, hammam, gardens, slow Provençal lunch. An optional taxi into Avignon old town in the late afternoon for a brief market stroll and a sit-down at a café terrace under the watchful walls of the Palais des Papes. Most evenings end with a long Provençal dinner at the Michelin-starred restaurant.

**Day two** is the countryside day with a private driver. We start with a brief stop at the cobblestone square of Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, then a long lunch at a vineyard estate restaurant — ratatouille, *tian*, tomato tart, Provençal beef daube, local rosé. The afternoon is **Carrières de Lumières**, an immersive art experience inside a former limestone quarry below Les Baux: monumental projected art moving across 14-metre walls, you sit on benches in the cool dark while van Gogh or Klimt paintings bloom around you. Then a drive past the dramatic hilltop fortress of Les Baux for the photo, no climbing required.

**Day three** is the vineyard morning. We head to **Châteauneuf-du-Pape** with a local sommelier — cellar tour and tasting at one of the prestige estates (Château La Nerthe, or Domaine du Vieux Télégraphe), then up to the ruins of the 14th-century papal summer château at the village summit. The view from the top runs across vineyards stretching to Mont Ventoux thirty kilometres away. The afternoon is slow at Auberge — pool, gardens, a final Provençal dinner.

What we're not doing in Provence: Aix-en-Provence (worth its own trip), the Luberon villages of Gordes and Roussillon (too cobblestone-heavy for our family), the lavender fields in bloom (wrong season — peak is late June through mid-July), and Pont du Gard or any other Roman ruin (we've seen the Roman ones in Turkey).

The point of Provence is the rhythm. Not how much you cover, but how slowly you cover it.

## The Riviera — the coastal half

![Aerial view of Nice and the French Riviera](https://images.pexels.com/photos/18071382/pexels-photo-18071382.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1600)

After Provence we head back to Nice for five nights at **Hyatt Regency Palais de la Méditerranée** on the Promenade des Anglais. A Belle Époque facade, indoor-outdoor pool, sea-view rooms, and ten minutes' walk into Old Nice.

Nice itself is a city, not a beach town — pastel-painted alleys, a famous open-air market at Cours Saleya, gelato shops that take their craft seriously, and a hilltop park (Castle Hill) with the best panoramic view of the bay and harbour. We'll do **Socca** at Lou Pilha Leva or René Socca — the local chickpea pancake from a wood-fired oven, eaten standing at a wooden counter — and a slow coffee at Café Indien in the old town. The trick to Castle Hill for anyone with mobility constraints: there's an elevator next to the I Love Nice sign that bypasses the 213 stairs.

But the most distinctive Riviera experiences are short train rides from Nice:

![Aerial view of Èze village above the Mediterranean](https://images.pexels.com/photos/7997666/pexels-photo-7997666.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1600)

**Èze.** A medieval village on a sea cliff 429 metres above the Mediterranean. The Jardin Exotique at the very top is a cactus garden with sea-cliff viewpoints — arguably the most photographed view on the entire Côte d'Azur. The village itself is steep cobblestones, but the gardens at the top are reachable by car or bus.

**Cap Ferrat.** A small peninsula that's been money for over a century. The Sentier du Littoral coastal path is a flat, paved sea-walk past quiet coves and discreet villas. The Villa Ephrussi de Rothschild's gardens at the top of the peninsula have terraces in nine themed styles and panoramic sea views in two directions.

**Villefranche-sur-Mer.** Seven minutes by train from Nice. A tiny medieval port painted in pastels, with a sandy beach (rare on the Riviera) and harbour-side restaurants. Sunset dinner here is one of the simpler pleasures of the coast.

**Antibes.** Twenty-five minutes by train. Port Vauban is where the world's largest superyachts dock — football-pitch boats with helipads — and the Marché Provençal in the old town is one of the better produce markets in the region.

The plan is to keep the Riviera light. Two and a half days of pacing it slowly, picking from the menu of options based on weather and energy.

## Monaco GP — the finale

![Aerial view of Monaco marina with luxury yachts](https://images.pexels.com/photos/31696410/pexels-photo-31696410.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1600)

The final stretch of the trip is two consecutive days at the 2026 Monaco Grand Prix.

**Sunday** is the race itself, watched from **Amazonico Terrace** — F1 Experiences' rooftop hospitality on top of Café de Paris Monte Carlo. Designated table seats, family-style lunch, beverage credit, and the F1 race itself unfolding in Casino Square ten metres below. The cars enter Casino Square at 280 km/h, brake hard, and fight for position through the right-hander. Race starts 15:00 CET, runs 78 laps, takes about two hours.

![Belle Époque architectural detail of the Monte Carlo Casino](https://images.pexels.com/photos/30687618/pexels-photo-30687618.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1600)

**Monday** is the post-GP day, when Casino Square reopens to pedestrians, the **Prince's Car Collection** at Terrasses de Fontvieille is back in business (~100 cars including Bugatti Type 35s, Sébastien Loeb's WRC Citroën, Charles Leclerc's Ferrari SF90), and the supercars that came for the race are still cruising the circuit. We walk the actual F1 track while it's being dismantled around us — past the swimming pool chicane, through the tunnel exit, around Casino Square itself with the barriers coming down.

The Sunday race is the bucket-list moment. The Monday is the wind-down — and probably the more relaxed of the two days, since there's no fixed schedule and you're just walking the most famous street circuit in the world.

## What we're choosing to skip

Some honest editing. The South of France could absorb three weeks easily. We have eleven days. Things we're skipping:

- **Saint-Tropez.** An hour and forty-five minutes from Nice each way, on coastal traffic. Famous for nightlife and celebrity yachts — wrong audience for us. The yacht-spotting at Antibes Port Vauban gives 80% of the experience at 25 minutes by train.
- **Cannes.** The 2026 Cannes Film Festival ends 23 May, before we arrive. Without the festival, Cannes is an upmarket Promenade des Anglais. We have one of those already.
- **Saturday Qualifying at the Monaco GP.** Widely considered more thrilling than the race, but adds a third 12-hour Monaco day to a sequence that already has two. We'll watch it on the room TV.
- **Aix-en-Provence.** Worth its own trip. Squeezing it in would compress Provence into a logistics exercise.
- **The Luberon villages — Gordes, Roussillon, Bonnieux.** Genuinely beautiful, but cobblestone-heavy and steep. They'll be there next time.
- **The Gorges du Verdon.** Two hours from Avignon, requires hiking. Not the trip's pace.
- **The Calanques near Cassis.** Same reason — coastal hiking. Stunning but demanding.
- **Lavender fields in bloom.** Peak bloom is late June through mid-July. We're three weeks early. The fields will be green, not purple.
- **Roman ruins (Pont du Gard, Arles, Nîmes).** We've seen Ephesus and the rest of the Turkish Roman heritage. The Provençal Roman sites would feel redundant for us.

The point of an editing list is to free up the trip for what matters most. Provence's countryside rhythm, the Riviera's coastal villages, the Monaco GP weekend. Three things, eleven days, no rushing.

Two anchor hotels (Auberge de Cassagne in Provence, Hyatt Regency in Nice), one TGV south and one TGV north, two Riviera coastal towns optional from Nice base, two Monaco days as the finale.

## Closing

Eleven days isn't enough to "do" the South of France — but it's enough to feel both halves of it. The Provençal half is the authentic France of vineyards, hilltop villages and Saturday markets. The Riviera half is theatre — coastline, Belle Époque hotels, panoramic views from Èze, the Monaco circuit at full song.

We'll write again after we're back, with real photos and real stories. For now, this is the anticipation.
